
_Translated from [[Solari]] and Aeikas_

# Fragment I: “On the Shaping of My Soul”

> **I write these words not for fame, nor praise, nor fortune,  
> but because the world cannot forget the vows that are carved onto the page.**  
> —Atiri

---

## **I. Of My Earliest Shadow**

I was first called a name not known.  
A name given, but lost, as one loses their youth, their soul.  
I remember losses more clearly than the name my mother whispered to me on the morning of my birth.  
Memory abandons kindness first; cruelty is loyal.

I knew nothing of the [[Buno Walls]] except the dust that gathered beneath them,  
and the noise of [[Setting/Settlements/Cities/Solaum|the city]] that lived on the other side;  
a world I believed distant as the stars.  
My parents were unremarkable, they told me.  
I remember little of them except the ordinary quiet of their lives.  
And perhaps it is right I remember so little.  
When the [[Setting/Cultures/Margos|Margos]] tore apart our door I did not understand  
that the fate of empires had already decided their death.

I was seven.  
Age enough to know fear, but not enough to understand malice.  
And so I learned both at once.

---

## **II. On Chains and the Holy City**

[[Itrea]] was beautiful.  
A cruel lesson: beauty and suffering often share a roof.  
The temples gleamed like the teeth of a beast that meant to swallow me.  
My masters beat me as one swats a fly  
—even when the priests and laws forbade it.  
But law is a thin shield when one holds no name.

They called me _horabos_.  
I began to believe it.  
The soul reshapes itself around the words others force upon it.

Yet within those same walls the gods of my ancestors found me again.  
I swept the temple floors,  
and in the scent of burned herbs I felt the faint breath of divinity—  
a reminder that even a slave may stand beneath the same sky as kings.

---

## **III. The Fire Within**

The first flame I summoned was a mistake.  
Most beginnings are.

It leapt from my hands in a moment of terror,  
when a blow intended to break my ribs instead birthed my awakening.  
I set the house aflame.  
I do not write this with pride.  
The fire was not justice; it was hunger.

My masters died.  
The city nearly burned with them.  
And I ran—  
not because I feared death,  
but because I feared I would become death.

The forest took me in.  
I knew nothing of spirits, winds, or balance.  
I knew only that wood burns.

---

## **IV. The Stranger of the Mountain**

When [[Varma]] found me,  
I was little more than a frightened beast with a spark in its chest.  
He chastised me for upsetting the harmony of the forest.  
I laughed,  
for I had never known that the trees possessed a mind  
or that the wind carried memory.

Varma was older than stone.  
I understood this not by the lines on his face  
but by the weight of his silence.  
He named me Aetor—the One Who Knows.  
In his tongue it meant reverence.  
In mine it became Atiri.  
A name I chose to keep because it reminded me  
that knowledge is not a gift but a burden.

---

## **V. Lessons of Dawn and Earth**

He woke me at every dawn.  
“Again,” he would say,  
even when my hands bled from drawing sigils in the dirt.  
His teaching was relentless, and therefore honest.

I learned of fire, of air, of the quiet tongues of trees,  
of the songs that mountains murmur beneath the crust of the world.  
I learned that magic is not power but perception.  
A man who can burn down a house is a danger,  
but a man who can hear the voice of a stone  
is a force.

Varma taught me that every leaf has a soul.  
I believed him only when I began to hear them whisper.

We climbed Mount Aemi together, countless times.  
He swore that on certain dawns the Goddess Aushuzi  
would touch her gaze to the peak.  
He felt her presence.  
I never did.  
Perhaps I was too mortal,  
or perhaps I listened too hard for something that was never meant for me.

---

## **VI. The Night Without Footprints**

On the 15th of Narimi,  
the wind remembered something I did not.  
Its breath was wrong—  
too cold, too hollow.

Varma did not greet me.  
He did not step across the moss,  
nor disturb a single branch.  
The world felt thinner,  
as if a great weight had been lifted  
not in relief  
but in rupture.

The plants bent in mourning.  
I do not know how I knew;  
I only know that they did.

I searched for him for forty days.  
I found nothing.  
Not a footprint.  
Not a strand of hair.  
A man whose presence could fill a valley  
left less trace than a passing breeze.

Perhaps he ascended.  
Perhaps he died.  
Perhaps he simply grew tired of teaching a stubborn boy  
who could not hear the gods.

Whatever the truth,  
emptiness is a teacher too.

---

## **VII. Return to the City of Stone**

I gathered what he had given me—  
the names of flames,  
the whispers of trees,  
the secrets that coil beneath the earth—  
and I walked toward Solaum,  
the city of my birth  
and the tomb of my past.

I returned not as _horabos_,  
but as Atiri.  
I returned not to seek a home,  
but to build a place  
where knowledge could live unchained.

If the world wished to forget my beginnings,  
let my writings ensure it never forgets my becoming.

# Fragment II: "The Lost Race"

## **I. The Coming of Varma, Who Was More Than Elf**

I lived two years by branch and cinder, believing myself a creature both cursed and chosen. Yet even the trees grew weary of my ignorance, for one day the fire I conjured to warm myself began devouring the forest with a hunger I could no longer restrain.

It was then that **Varma** found me.

He appeared without sound, as elves do, though there was something in him that was not elven alone. His eyes contained the calm of ancient lakes and the cruelty of high mountain winds. He looked not at my body, but through it, as though measuring the disturbance my mana caused in the air.

“You imbalance the forest,” he said, voice cool as moonlight. “And you invite spirits you cannot name.”

I did not yet believe in spirits.  
Varma did not yet believe in me.

Still, he did not kill me.  
He asked for my name.

I had none.

He frowned, as though this absence offended him, and after a long silence he said in his own tongue, “Then I shall call you **Aetor**—The One Who Knows.”

The Solari tongue softened it to **Atiri**, and thus I was reborn a second time.

Varma’s teachings were not gentle.  
He woke me each dawn.  
He made me practice until the moon climbed high.  
He fed me only when he remembered hunger.  
He spoke in riddles and silences, and every lesson was carved into me like script into bronze.

But under him I learned the language of trees, the breathing of mountains, the patience of rivers, the cruelty of storms.  
I learned the old magics—draconic tongues that tasted of smoke and iron, nature-spells that hummed with the pulse of the world, and elemental arts that threatened to hollow me out with their enormity.

He poured knowledge into me as though he sought to empty himself.

And sometimes, in the quiet between words, I wondered whether he feared what waited for him when he became empty.

## **II. The Ascent and the Dawn That Never Answered Me**

Varma taught that every stone possesses memory, every breeze intention, every star a cold, watchful spirit. I accepted his teaching, though rarely did I feel what he claimed to feel.

One morning before the sun, he dragged me up Mount Aemi.  
“Today,” he said, “Aushuzi walks the sky. If you open your spirit, you may feel her pass.”

We sat in silence as the eastern wind stirred the snow.  
Varma’s posture softened; his breath slowed.  
He whispered that the Goddess of Dawn was near.

I felt nothing.

But I continued to climb the mountain at each turn of the east wind, year after year, dawn after dawn. The peak became my second teacher. In its silence I learned humility. In its cold I learned endurance. In its loneliness I learned truth.

Yet Aushuzi never came to me.

Varma said,  
“You are not yet aligned with the world.”

I wondered if the world itself rejected me, as so many others had.

## **III. The Day the World Emptied**

On the fifteenth day of Narimi, in the year 412 IOG—the day the moon of war hangs highest—Varma vanished.

I woke before dawn and knew instantly that something had changed. The air felt hollow. The plants drooped in ways no natural wind could cause. Even the insects refused to sing.

I called for him.  
No answer.

I searched the clearing.  
No footprints.

I reached into the weave of mana around us and found only absence, as though a great weight once pressed upon the world and had suddenly lifted.

For forty-five years he had greeted me every morning.  
For forty-five years he had shaped me, broken me, rebuilt me.  
And then he was gone—  
not departed,  
not killed,  
but _erased_.

I waited for him for many days.  
I waited for him for many nights.  
He did not return.

Only when the emptiness itself began to choke me did I descend the mountain, carrying his teachings, his riddles, and the unwanted inheritance of his silence.

## IV. On Varma, The Last of The Astral Elves

I have written before of Varma as my master, but that name alone is a veil over a truth far older and heavier than any mortal title. For Varma was not merely Aeian, nor Solari, nor of any people who walk this earth now. **He was one of the Astral Elves**, the last flickering ember of a lineage that once bridged worlds and spoke with the same ease to stars as we speak to stone.

I did not understand this when he found me.  
I understood little of anything then.

In those days I thought him only a strange forest hermit—too old to be young, too young to be old, with a gaze that felt like cold water poured into the cracks of my soul. I knew nothing of the Astral Courts or the cosmic diplomacy they once held and nothing of the ancient event in which his kin was extinguished.

Only after years under his tutelage did I begin to notice what others could not:  
how he cast no shadow when the moons aligned;  
how starlight bent minutely when it passed him;  
how the wind quieted as if to listen when he spoke of the heavens.

He moved with a patience no mortal blood possesses.  
He waited with the ease of someone who had waited centuries before learning to regret it.

He spoke so rarely of his people that each word he offered was a shard of a shattered mirror. From these shards I pieced together fragments of a truth too large to hold:

* That their spirits were tuned to the heavens like instruments of crystalline resonance.  
* That they once guided the exchanges between gods, mortals, and the astral powers that lie beyond the veil of our knowing. 
* And that, in the end, they were betrayed by the gods and the people of this plane, as were many others.  

When he taught me the old magics—those that tasted of starlight and mountain breath—I felt, for the briefest moments, the echo of an age when beings like him shaped destinies with a gesture. I felt the unbearable loneliness that lived beneath his calm. And though I was young, I sensed that my master carried the weight of an entire dead people upon his back.

He once told me, during a night when the stars hung low and bright like embers drifting across black water:  
“Aetor, listen closely. When you stare at the heavens long enough, you will hear what has been lost. And when you hear it, you will understand why silence frightens gods.”

I have stared often since.  
I still do not understand what he meant.

But I know this:  
Varma was not merely my teacher.  
He was the last witness to an age erased.  
He was a blade honed by grief, a scholar shaped by eternity, and a wanderer who walked among mortals as one walks among dreams—half here, half elsewhere.

If any magic of mine endures beyond my death, let it be this truth:

**Varma is the Last of the Astral Elves**
